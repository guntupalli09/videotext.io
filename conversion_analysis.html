The three largest leaks

Leak 1 · Visitor → Signup (−70%)752 of 1,078 visitors never signed up. But this is not the primary leak it looks like — Part 4 shows 84 users completed a transcript job with no account at all, and "Transcript → Signup" (75 users) is the single most common path. VideoText lets people get value before creating an account, so a chunk of this drop-off is people who already got what they came for anonymously.
Leak 2 · Checkout reached → Payment completed (−91%, post-June data)Since checkout_session_created was instrumented (2026‑06‑17), 33 people reached Stripe checkout and only 3 completed payment. This is the single steepest, most confidently-measured drop in the entire funnel and the highest-leverage fix candidate — see Part 6.
Leak 3 · First job → Second job (−72%)Only 95 of 338 first-time tool users came back for a second job. Most visitors are one-and-done: they solve one transcription/subtitle task and leave, never generating the repeat engagement that would put them near a paywall.

Timing: when converters move, they move fast

Median time between adjacent steps (signup→first job, first job→pricing, pricing→upgrade click, upgrade click→checkout, checkout→payment) is effectively same-session (≈0 minutes to a few hours) for the users who do convert at each step. There is no evidence of long, multi-day deliberation loops between steps — the friction is binary (convert now, in-session, or don't), not a slow-drip nurture problem. This has a direct implication for Part 10: in-session prompts and fixing checkout friction will move the needle faster than long email drip campaigns.

Repeat pricing-page visits are the single strongest behavioral signal of intent to buy in this dataset. Converters view the pricing page ~4x more often than non-converters (4.89 vs 1.22 visits). Of 176 total pricing viewers, 34 (19%) came back more than once — that group is disproportionately where the 18 buyers came from.

158 pricing viewers (90%) never paid. Of those, 41 (26%) went on to keep using the free tool afterward — evidence they didn't abandon the product, they specifically declined the paid plan and downgraded their intent to "stay free." The remaining ~117 non-converted pricing viewers show no further product usage in the window — closer to permanent abandonment, though we cannot distinguish "decided against it" from "still evaluating, hasn't returned yet" without a longer observation window or churn survey.

PART 6

Payment friction

Highest-confidence finding in this reportcheckout_session_created was only instrumented starting 2026‑06‑17 (23 of the 26 lifetime payment_completed events happened before that date, with no checkout-stage visibility). In the properly-instrumented window since June 17: 33 users reached Stripe checkout, and only 3 completed payment — a 91% checkout-stage abandonment rate. This is far higher than the click→checkout drop (upgrade click → checkout ≈ 46% of clickers) and is the steepest true drop-off measured anywhere in the funnel.

What we can and can't say about why:

  • Not a technical failure problem. Only 4 users ever saw a payment_failed event, and all 4 of them eventually completed payment anyway (0 of 4 were lost to failure) — Stripe-side technical failures are not the driver of the 91% checkout abandonment.
  • Plan selection happens before checkout, and it's already lossy. plan_clicked fired for 30 users but checkout_session_created for 33 — roughly consistent, meaning most people who pick a plan do reach Stripe. The loss is concentrated at or inside Stripe, not before it.
  • No confirmed evidence of "repeated comparison" behavior at checkout (e.g., re-opening checkout multiple times before abandoning) — the event volume (37 checkout_started, 41 checkout_session_created events for 33 users) shows some multi-attempt behavior (avg ~1.2 checkout events per user) but is too sparse to characterize as hesitation vs. simple retries.
  • Hypothesis, not evidence: a 91% drop between "Stripe session created" and "payment completed" this severe is more typical of price-shock at the final screen, unexpected currency/tax addition, a broken/slow redirect, or a plan-price mismatch between what was clicked and what Stripe shows — but PostHog has no event capturing what happened inside the Stripe-hosted page, so the specific cause cannot be confirmed from this data alone. This is the single most important tracking gap to close (see Part 12).
  • Churn after purchase is also real: 6 of 18 paying customers (33%) triggered subscription_deleted within the window — a meaningful post-purchase leak in addition to the pre-purchase one.

PART 9

Customer psychology — why users decided not to upgrade

Each reason below is graded by confidence and tied to a specific behavioral signal. Reasons without direct evidence in this dataset are explicitly marked as unsupported.

Checkout-stage friction / distrust at the payment step

High confidence

91% of users who reached Stripe checkout since June 17 did not complete payment (30/33), with technical failure ruled out (all 4 payment_failed users eventually succeeded). Something at or inside the checkout page — price surprise, unclear terms, trust signals, currency confusion — is turning away people who had already decided to click "upgrade" and select a plan.

Affects ~91% of checkout-reachers

Free plan is generous enough to cover most people's actual need

High confidence

15 power users with 5+ completed jobs never upgraded, and usage is bursty (avg 3.3 active days per user) rather than sustained — consistent with the free tier fully absorbing a one-time or occasional batch task. Only 71 of 1,078 visitors (6.6%) ever clicked an upgrade button at all.

Affects the majority of the 158 non-converted pricing viewers

One-time / task-based usage, not a recurring need

High confidence

84 users completed a transcript job with no signup at all, and the dominant journey shape is "one tool run → maybe signup → leave." Free-tier tool users average only 1.7–1.4 jobs vs. 4.7–1.9 for payers — most usage looks like solving a single task, not building a workflow.

Likely explains a large share of the 752 visitors who never signed up and the 41 pricing-viewers who stayed on free

Geography / willingness-to-pay in USD-priced markets

High confidence

India (102 visitors), Germany (57), Philippines (42), UK (25), China (25) — 251 visitors, 23% of traffic — produced zero paid conversions. 12 of 15 unpaid power users are outside the US. The US converts at 3.7% vs. ~0–1% for most other high-volume countries.

Affects ~20-25% of total traffic

Weak/under-tested call-to-action placement

Medium confidence

Only 71 of 176 pricing-page viewers (40%) and only 39 of 246 transcript-tool users (16%) ever clicked an upgrade button, despite the tool page generating more upgrade clicks than the pricing page itself. This is consistent with the CTA being present but not compelling enough, though we can't isolate copy/design from price/need without a test.

Estimated 15-25% of engaged users

Post-purchase trust/value gap (churn)

Medium confidence

6 of 18 paying customers (33%) triggered subscription_deleted within the window. This isn't a reason people don't start paying, but it is hard evidence that even converted users aren't reliably retained — a value or expectations gap after purchase.

33% of paying customers

Pricing too expensive / confusing pricing / missing feature / enterprise needs

Not supported by current data

PostHog captures no pricing-page scroll depth, no plan-comparison interaction, no exit survey, and no "why did you cancel" reason field on subscription_deleted. These are plausible, common SaaS objections, but nothing in this dataset confirms or sizes them — see Part 12 for exactly what tracking would resolve this.

PART 12

Missing data & tracking gaps

  • Checkout-stage detail. No events for: plan/price displayed at checkout, payment form field interaction, currency shown, coupon/promo attempted, or explicit "checkout abandoned" (only inferred by absence of payment_completed). This is the single highest-priority gap — it blocks diagnosing the 91% checkout drop.
  • Cancellation reason. subscription_deleted carries no reason/feedback property — churn cause (price, missing feature, one-time need, dissatisfaction) is currently unknowable.
  • Consistent identity across anonymous → signed-up transition. signup_completed/google_signup_completed only exist from 2026-04-03 onward, while job_completed and pageview data go back to Feb 2026 — pre-April users and their conversion cannot be reliably funneled. Confirm person-merge on signup is correctly stitching anonymous pre-signup activity to the identified person for all auth methods (email OTP, Google, magic link).
  • Free-tier limit / quota events. No event fires when a user hits or approaches their free-tier usage limit — this is presumably the main paywall trigger and should be explicitly tracked (limit_reached, quota_warning_shown) separately from the generic paywall_shown/upgrade_prompt_seen.
  • Pricing page interaction depth. No scroll depth, plan-comparison-toggle, or FAQ-expand events on /pricing — can't distinguish careful evaluation from confusion or quick bounce among the 158 non-converted viewers.
  • Currency / billing region on payment events. payment_completed/payment_failed carry no currency or local-price property, making it impossible to verify whether non-US non-conversion is price-driven without inferring from country alone.
  • Revenue amount on payment_completed. No plan price/MRR value was found on payment events in the schema explored — revenue-by-tool and revenue-by-segment (explicitly requested in Part 7/8) cannot be computed without this.
  • Tool taxonomy is incomplete. Only two tool_type values exist (video-to-subtitles, video-to-transcript) — translate, voice, and other tools referenced in the task brief either don't exist yet or aren't tagging tool_type; if they exist in-product, they need this property added.
  • Company/team size, use-case, or persona properties. Nothing captures whether a user is an individual, student, agency, or enterprise buyer — Part 9's "enterprise needs" hypothesis is untestable without this.
  • NPS/CSAT or in-product feedback events. No qualitative signal anywhere in the schema — every psychology conclusion in Part 9 is inferred from behavior, never confirmed by a user's stated reason.
FINAL REPORT

Executive summary

1. Why aren't more users upgrading?

Three forces compound: (a) most visitors get what they need from a single anonymous tool run and never build a recurring habit that would justify paying; (b) of the minority who do show real purchase intent (click upgrade, reach checkout), the large majority are lost at the Stripe checkout screen itself, not earlier in consideration; and (c) roughly a quarter of all traffic comes from countries where USD SaaS pricing converts near zero.

2. What evidence supports each reason?

One-time usage: 84 users complete a job with no account at all; free-tier job counts (1.4-1.7 avg) are a fraction of paid-user job counts (1.9-4.7 avg); only 95 of 338 first-time tool users return for a second job. Checkout drop: 33 Stripe checkout sessions since June 17 → only 3 payments, with technical failure ruled out. Geography: India/Germany/Philippines/UK/China (23% of traffic) show 0% conversion vs. 3.7% for the US.

3. Biggest leaks in the funnel

Ranked by how confidently they're measured and how close the affected users are to paying: (1) Checkout reached → payment completed, −91% — highest confidence, highest urgency, since these users already decided to buy. (2) First job → second job, −72% — the volume leak that keeps most users from ever generating upgrade intent. (3) Visitor → signup, −70% — partly an artifact of the no-signup-required product design rather than pure loss.

4. Which users are closest to becoming paid customers?

Two groups: the 34 repeat pricing-page visitors (avg 4.89 visits among converters vs. 1.22 among non-converters — this is the strongest buy-intent signal in the data), and the 30 users who abandoned Stripe checkout since June — they clicked upgrade, picked a plan, and reached the payment screen. Both groups are small, known, and re-engageable today.

5. Which changes would most likely double paid conversions?

Fixing checkout-stage abandonment alone — recovering even a third of the 30 lost post-checkout users — would roughly double the 18 total payments observed in this window. It is also the fastest fix: it requires no pricing strategy change, no new feature, just diagnosing and repairing an existing step users already chose to take.

6. Next 30-day strategy, as founder

  1. Week 1: Instrument the checkout funnel properly (plan/price shown, payment form started, error states) and watch 10 session replays of recent checkout abandoners. Ship whatever the replays reveal (likely price display, currency, or trust/UX issue) immediately — don't wait for a full experiment cycle on something this clear-cut.
  2. Week 2: Add a cancellation-reason prompt to the cancel flow, and personally reach out (email or in-app) to the 30 checkout-abandoners with a direct, no-pressure "did something go wrong?" — recovering even a handful pays for the month.
  3. Week 3: Ship a geography-aware price test (adaptive/PPP pricing) for the top non-converting countries, and tighten the in-tool upgrade CTA on /video-to-transcript, the highest-intent surface already in the product.
  4. Week 4: Add a usage-based upgrade nudge triggered at a user's 2nd completed job (the point where the funnel shows the steepest volume drop), and start capturing plan revenue/currency on payment events so next month's analysis can size actual dollars, not just user counts.
