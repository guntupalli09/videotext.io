Who VideoText is actually built for, according to the data

A ground-up segmentation of every active user based on what they did, not what we assumed they'd do. No persona field exists in this dataset — every conclusion below is inferred from tool choice, file characteristics, repeat behavior, and payment events.

Window: Feb 11 – Jul 26, 2026 (~5.5 months) Active users (≥1 job): 973 Total events analyzed: 70,888 Paying accounts: 18
0. Data reality check 1. Segments 2. Comparison 3. Unexpected use 4. Six answers 5. What to instrument next

0What the data can and can't tell you

Before segmenting anyone, it's worth being blunt about the instrumentation as it stands today, because it shapes every conclusion below.

There is no persona, role, or use-case field anywhere in the schema. No "what do you use this for" onboarding question, no job title, no company/team property, no seats, no industry. Every label in this report (transcript-extractor, subtitle-fixer, etc.) is a behavioral cluster name I assigned based on tool choice and file characteristics — not a self-reported identity. Treat labels like "students" or "journalists" as plausible interpretations, not confirmed facts.

87.5% of active users (851 of 973) are functionally anonymous — no plan, no email, no country, no referrer captured on their person profile. They ran a job and PostHog never saw an identify call attached to them. This means "conversion to paid" and "retention" can only be measured reliably for the 122 identified accounts (12.5% of active users) — the anonymous majority is a black box beyond job count and tool choice.

Revenue amounts aren't tracked at all. $virt_revenue and $virt_mrr are $0 on every single person, and payment_completed carries no amount/currency property. I can tell you who paid and how often they came back, but not how much money any segment generated. LTV comparisons below are therefore expressed in engagement terms (jobs, active days), not dollars.

One account (santhoshguntupalli06@gmail.com) looks like internal/founder testing — 118 jobs, 75 active days, 52 of the platform's 91 total YouTube-transcript jobs, and 4 of the 18 paid conversions. It's excluded from every segment statistic below so it doesn't distort what a real customer looks like.

What each segment is probably made of, and why

Transcript extractors (51.4%, the largest segment by far). They upload a video or paste a YouTube link and want text back — no captions, no burned-in styling. Average source file for this workflow is ~149 MB, consistent with full-length recordings (lectures, interviews, meetings, long videos) rather than short social clips. 35 of these users used the YouTube-URL path specifically — extracting someone else's video as text, which is a research/reference behavior, not a "caption my own content" behavior. Best-fit real-world identities: researchers, students, journalists pulling quotes, note-takers, content repurposers — not the classic "YouTuber" persona.

Subtitle fixers (26.7%). Average uploaded file is 187 KB — a subtitle file, not a video. These users are not generating captions in VideoText; they're bringing an SRT/VTT they already have (auto-captions from YouTube itself, Zoom, another tool) and using VideoText purely as a cleanup/correction utility. This is a standalone workflow the product wasn't necessarily built to be used as.

Subtitle creators (17.6%). The segment closest to the assumed ICP — upload video, get burned-in or exportable captions. Source files average ~153 MB, statistically indistinguishable in size from the transcript segment, so file length doesn't separate "creator" from "researcher" — tool choice does.

Translators (3.3%). Average input ~6 MB — again a subtitle file, not a video. Like the fixers, these users skip VideoText's own subtitle-generation step and only touch it for translation of subtitles produced elsewhere. Likely multilingual creators or small localization operators working across several source tools.

Retention and paid conversion both point the same direction, and it's not the assumed one. Transcript extractors are the only segment with a non-trivial retention rate (8.2% return on a second day vs. 4–6% everywhere else) and the only segment with meaningful paid conversion (2.6%, vs. 0.0–0.4% for fixers, creators, and translators). Of the 10 paying accounts that also appear in the job-activity data, 9 are transcript-dominant. Every pro-plan account in the top-15 most active users is transcript-dominant except one fixer. Subtitle creators — the segment that looks most like "the target audience" — has zero paid conversions in this dataset.

Lifetime engagement value concentrates in a tiny group. Only 24 users (2.5% of active users) reached 5+ jobs; only 8 reached 10+. Among the highest-volume repeat users, transcript-family users dominate (7 of the top 15), including the two longest-lifespan accounts (48 and 108 days between first and last job). Without dollar revenue, "LTV" here means sustained engagement — and by that measure, transcript extraction is the only segment showing real compounding usage.

Most underserved segment

Subtitle creators. They're the second-largest segment (171 users), growing the fastest of any tool, and structurally the closest match to a "content creator" ICP — yet they convert to paid at 0% and retain barely above one-and-done (4.1%, lowest of any segment with volume). That combination — high growth, zero monetization — is the clearest sign of an underserved segment: people want the subtitle workflow enough to keep showing up in growing numbers, but something in the free→paid path or the output quality isn't giving them a reason to convert or come back. Translators show the same pattern at smaller scale (0% paid, 6.2% retained).

3Yes — usage looks meaningfully different from the assumed product

If VideoText was built around "upload your video, get subtitles," the assumed core loop is video-to-subtitles. That workflow accounts for only 17.6% of active users. Three clear "off-label" patterns show up in the data:

①Transcription used as a standalone note-taking/research tool, not a subtitle input

Size: 500 users, 51.4% of the active base — the single largest pattern in the product. These users never touch subtitle generation; they take a transcript and leave (avg 0.18 downloads, 0.06 transcript copies per user — most don't even copy or download, suggesting they read it in-page and move on). 35 of them use the dedicated YouTube-URL path, meaning they're extracting text from someone else's video, not their own footage — a research/reference behavior, structurally incompatible with a "caption your video" persona.

Why they'd prefer this: turning spoken content into searchable, quotable, copy-pasteable text is a faster way to extract information than watching or re-watching a video — useful for research, studying, journalism, or repurposing someone else's content into notes or an article.

Recommendation: market to them directly. This is not a side effect to tolerate — it's the majority workflow and the only one with above-floor retention and paid conversion. A "YouTube/video → text" positioning aimed at researchers, students, and content-repurposers deserves at least equal billing with subtitle-focused messaging, not a footnote.

②Subtitle fixing as a standalone workflow, disconnected from subtitle generation

Size: 260 users, 26.7% of the active base. Average uploaded file for this tool is 187 KB — a subtitle file, not video. These users generated captions somewhere else (YouTube auto-captions, Zoom, another editor) and only came to VideoText to clean up errors.

Why they'd prefer this: auto-caption tools everywhere produce serviceable-but-flawed subtitles; a fast, dedicated fixer is a lower-friction ask than re-generating from scratch, especially if their video lives elsewhere and re-uploading it is undesirable or impossible.

Recommendation: don't over-invest yet. It's the second-largest cluster and growing fast, but it converts at 0.4% paid and barely retains (4.6%). It looks like a genuine utility need, but today it behaves like a single-use tool, not a monetizable workflow — worth a lightweight, separately-branded "fix my captions" entry point to capture the traffic, without diverting core roadmap investment until conversion data says otherwise.

③Translation used without ever generating subtitles in VideoText

Size: 32 users, 3.3% of the active base — small, but a real, repeatable pattern. Average input file ~6 MB, again pointing to subtitle files rather than raw video.

Why they'd prefer this: multilingual creators and small localization operators often already have captions from a platform's native tools or a different vendor and only need one specific step — translation — rather than the full pipeline.

Recommendation: instrument before deciding. Too small a sample (32 users, 0 paid) to justify a dedicated go-to-market push today, but the standalone-translation behavior is a legitimate signal for a "localization" product surface later if it keeps growing.

4Direct answers

1Who is VideoText actually built for today?

By behavior, not by design intent: a video/audio-to-text extraction tool used mostly by people who want the words out of a recording — their own or someone else's — rather than a captioning tool used by video creators. Half the active base never generates a subtitle at all.

2Who receives the most value?

Transcript extractors — they're the only segment with retention meaningfully above "used it once and left" (8.2% vs. 4–6%) and the only one with real repeat/power users among the top-15 most active accounts.

3Who is most willing to pay?

Transcript extractors, decisively: 2.6% paid conversion vs. 0.0–0.4% everywhere else, and 9 of the 10 paying accounts traceable in job data are transcript-dominant.

4Which segment should become the primary ICP?

Transcript extraction, reframed as a product for people who need text from spoken content — researchers, students, journalists, note-takers, content repurposers pulling from their own or others' (including YouTube) video. It's the largest segment, the only one retaining, and the only one converting to paid. The YouTube-URL sub-pattern (35 users) is worth its own landing page.

5Which segment should we stop actively targeting?

Not "stop targeting" outright — none show negative signal, just weak monetization — but subtitle fixing and translation should stop being roadmap priorities until conversion improves. They're standalone, single-session utility behaviors (0.4% and 0.0% paid) that read as "nice to have available," not segments to build features or messaging around right now.

6How should homepage, pricing, onboarding, messaging, and roadmap change?

Homepage: lead with "turn any video into text" (transcript-first), with YouTube-link input as a first-class entry point alongside file upload — not buried under a subtitles-first hero. Pricing: the paywall is currently reached by 145 users (14.9% of active users saw paywall_shown) but only 11 upgraded — instrument which segment hits the paywall and tune the free-tier job/minute limits around transcript users' actual usage pattern (short bursts, ~1–2 jobs per visit) rather than a one-size limit. Onboarding: add a single explicit "what are you trying to do?" question at signup — it's the cheapest fix for the biggest blind spot in this entire analysis, since right now no field distinguishes a researcher from a caption-buyer. Messaging: speak to extraction and note-taking ("get the transcript," "search inside your videos," "quote it, don't rewatch it") as the primary hook, with subtitle/caption generation as a secondary, still-important use case. Feature prioritization: invest in what transcript users actually do with output (search, copy, export to notes tools) over further subtitle-styling polish, since styling serves the smallest, least-monetizing segment.

5What to instrument to answer this with confidence next time

• A self-reported use-case field at signup ("What will you use VideoText for?") — the single highest-leverage addition; every label in this report is currently inferred, not confirmed.

• Revenue amount and currency on payment_completed, plus a working $virt_revenue/$virt_mrr pipeline — without this, no segment's dollar LTV can be computed, only engagement proxies.

• Identity resolution across anonymous → signed-up state. 8 of 18 paying accounts have no matching job_created event in this window, meaning their pre-signup activity (if any) is on a different, unmerged person profile. Fix the identify-call timing so job history survives signup.

• Company/team or seat-count property — there is currently zero evidence for or against "marketing agencies" or "enterprise," because no field distinguishes an individual from an organizational account. Add it before making any enterprise-tier decisions.

• Video duration or source-language/target-language properties on jobs — file size is a rough proxy for content length and says nothing about spoken language, which matters directly for the localization/translator segment.

• A distinct-download/export-destination property on result_downloaded (e.g., SRT vs. TXT vs. "copied to clipboard") to tell subtitle-bound exports apart from note-taking-bound exports with certainty, instead of inferring it from tool choice.
